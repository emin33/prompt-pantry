# Preliminary Research: Slow-Cooker Olive Garden-Style Pasta e Fagioli (Hamilton Beach 33969A)

## TL;DR
- A truly dump-and-go execution is achievable but requires three non-negotiable concessions: use 90/10 (or leaner) ground beef to avoid grease pooling, add ditalini in the last 30 minutes on HIGH only, and start with roughly 25–33% less liquid than stovetop copycat recipes call for.
- The "always brown the beef first" orthodoxy is real but overstated for a tomato-broth soup; lean raw beef + an aggressive break-up at the start + an end-of-cook skim gets you 80–90% of the flavor at zero stove friction.
- The 33969A's temperature probe is irrelevant for this dish — it's a meat-cut feature, and pasta/beans/ground beef cook on time, not on probe temp.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Browning Question — Skippable, with caveats
- **Consensus among recipe authors is "brown first."** Every major Olive Garden-style copycat slow-cooker recipe surveyed (Damn Delicious, The Recipe Critic, Chelsea's Messy Apron, Persnickety Plates, Averie Cooks, Taste of Home, Spend With Pennies, $5 Dinners, Southern Plate, Joyous Apron, Tastes of Lizzy T, Simple Nourished Living) calls for skillet-browning the meat before transfer. The cited rationales are flavor (Maillard), texture ("better crumble"), and grease removal.
- **Authoritative sources hedge but don't categorically forbid raw-dump.** America's Test Kitchen's "Slow-Cooker Classic Beef Chili" recipe by Dan Zuccarello (americastestkitchen.com/recipes/10460) reports: "Browning the beef to develop flavor is standard in most traditional chilis, but this produced overcooked, gritty meat in the slow cooker. To fix this, we found mixing the raw beef with a panade — a mixture of bread and milk often used in meatballs — before browning it worked wonders." ATK's tested fix is leaner beef (85% lean) and not overbrowning — implying raw beef in the cooker isn't a clean win either way. The USDA explicitly notes color is not a doneness indicator: "At 160°F, a safely cooked patty may look brown, pink or some variation of brown or pink." This undermines casual claims that raw-dumped beef looks "gray" and therefore tastes bad — those are aesthetic, not safety, judgments.
- **Substantial counterevidence exists.** Melanie Cooks' raw-dump slow-cooker chili has hundreds of positive user comments specifically praising the no-brown shortcut; she explicitly recommends extra-lean ground beef so "there is no fat to drain." Effortless Foodie's no-browning chili con carne reports "meltingly tender mince at the end of cooking. I almost never bother browning mince for slow cooker recipes." Show Me the Yummy goes further and argues pre-browning produces *worse* texture: "results in over-cooked, liquidly meat, with very little texture."
- **The hinge variable is fat content, not browning.** Every author who endorses raw-dump specifies lean ground beef (90% or 93%); every author warning against it implicitly assumes 80/20. With 80/20 dumped raw, multiple sources document visible fat pooling and a "greasy" tomato broth (The Lazy Slow Cooker, The Seasoned Mom, Lemons & Zest). With 90/10 or 93/7, the rendered fat is minimal enough to be ignored or skimmed with a spoon at the end.
- **Texture claims to discount:** "Rubbery" doesn't appear in any authoritative source. The honest descriptors are "soft," "clumped," "tender," or "dry/gritty" if overcooked. The dominant real failure mode of raw-dumped beef is that it forms one big mass unless physically broken up at the start and stirred at least once during cooking (My Fearless Kitchen, Fit as a Mama Bear, The Lazy Slow Cooker all flag this — a potato masher, Mix 'n Chop, or sturdy spoon is sufficient).
- **Frozen pre-cooked crumbles (option a):** Viable but rarely discussed in pasta e fagioli copycats specifically. They sidestep the grease and browning problem entirely but cost more per pound and tend toward a homogenous, slightly bouncy texture. UNL Food's freezer-crumble guidance notes frozen pre-cooked beef should be used within 2-3 months for best flavor.
- **"Browning in the slow cooker on HIGH before adding liquids" (option d):** The Lazy Slow Cooker documents a workable version — dry-load the cooker with raw beef on HIGH for 2 hours, breaking up periodically, then drain rendered fat before adding the rest. This violates "dump and go" — it still requires three lid-lifts to break and stir, plus a drain step — and is therefore not a clear win for this audience.
- **Verdict for the recipe:** Lean ground beef (90/10 ideally, 93/7 acceptable if you stir more aggressively) dumped raw, broken up at load, with one mid-cook stir to break clumps, is the best effort-to-flavor ratio. The flavor loss vs. skillet-browned is real but small in a tomato-forward, herb-heavy, vinegar-finished broth where Maillard notes are masked anyway.

### 2. Bean Handling — Canned, drained, added with the pasta
- **Canned is unambiguously correct** for this build. The FDA's "Bad Bug Book," along with university extensions (K-State, Illinois Extension, USU Extension), explicitly warns against cooking dried red kidney beans in a slow cooker without first boiling them on the stovetop for at least 10 minutes (30 minutes recommended) to destroy phytohaemagglutinin. Per the FDA chapter (cited verbatim by K-State Extension and Food Network): "Raw kidney beans contain from 20,000 to 70,000 hau, while fully cooked beans contain from 200 to 400 hau." Per Wikipedia's Phytohaemagglutinin article tracing to the FDA Bad Bug Book: "Insufficient cooking, such as in a slow cooker at 75 °C / 167 °F, may not completely destroy the toxins" — corroborated by independent temperature testing (The Default Cook) showing Hamilton Beach on LOW runs "in the mid to high 160s." Eating as few as four undercooked beans can trigger gastroenteritis. Canned beans are pre-cooked at commercial canning temperatures and are safe straight from the can.
- **Convergence on bean varieties:** Cannellini (or great northern) + dark red kidney is the dominant Olive Garden-style pairing. Five+ surveyed recipes use exactly this pair (Damn Delicious, Chelsea's Messy Apron, Cooking Classy, Persnickety Plates, The Recipe Critic).
- **Timing — add early vs. late:** Recipes split. Roughly half (Damn Delicious 2020, Adventures of a Nurse, Cooking Classy as adapted, Tastes of Lizzy T, Southern Plate) add beans at the start with everything else; the other half (The Recipe Critic, Evelyn's Cooking, Insanely Good, Averie Cooks) add beans in the final 30 minutes with the pasta. Late addition keeps beans intact; early addition lets them absorb tomato flavor but risks them breaking down into broth-thickener. For an Olive Garden look — visible whole beans in broth — late addition wins. The convenience cost is one lid lift, which can be combined with the mandatory pasta addition.
- **Salt note:** Pre-rinsing canned beans is meaningful for sodium control. Per Jones & Mount (University of Tennessee, Knoxville, 2009, presented at the Institute of Food Technologists Conference as "Sodium Reduction in Canned Bean Varieties by Draining and Rinsing"): "the draining treatment alone reduced sodium by 36% while the draining-rinsing treatment reduced sodium by 41%." Rinsing also removes the slightly slimy can-liquid, both of which matter when the broth is already concentrated by long cooking.

### 3. The Pasta Problem — Dry pasta, last 30 minutes on HIGH, lid stays on
- **Unanimous consensus across copycat recipes:** Add uncooked ditalini in the final 25–30 minutes on HIGH. Damn Delicious, Persnickety Plates, Averie Cooks, The Recipe Critic, Upstate Ramblings, Joyous Apron, Maebells, $5 Dinners, and Taste of Home all use the same window. This is the only widely-tested approach that meets the one-pot constraint.
- **Documented failure mode #1: Pasta cooked the full 6–8 hours = mush.** Universally flagged. Even Italian-cookery sources (Barilla's own slow-cooker recipes) prefer to cook pasta separately or add at the very end.
- **Documented failure mode #2: Uneven pasta if not stirred when added.** Joyous Apron and others recommend stirring immediately after dropping in the dry pasta to prevent clumping at the bottom or floating mats on top. This is one allowable lid-lift, justified.
- **Documented failure mode #3: 30 minutes on LOW is not enough.** Pasta needs the higher heat to actually cook through; recipes that try to keep the slow cooker on LOW for this step report undercooked, gummy ditalini. If the cooker has been running on LOW for the soup phase, switch to HIGH when the pasta goes in.
- **Quantity matters.** Most Olive Garden copycats use 1 to 1.5 cups dry ditalini (roughly 6–8 oz) for a recipe yielding ~3 quarts of finished soup. Pushing higher leads to "stew, not soup" complaints in user comments.
- **Cook the pasta separately and ladle soup over it (option d):** Recommended by 365 Days of Crockpot, Spend With Pennies, Salt & Lavender, and many user commenters specifically when leftovers are expected. It violates the one-pot constraint but is the only method that prevents leftover bloat. Worth flagging as a user-choice optional path rather than the default.

### 4. Liquid Ratios — Cut stovetop liquid by ~25–33%
- **Confirmed evaporation differential.** Hamilton Beach's own manual states: "Slow cookers allow for very little evaporation. If making your favorite soup, stew, or sauce, reduce the liquid called for in the original recipe. If too thick, liquid can be added later." Better Than Bouillon's own slow-cooker guidance echoes this: "There's no evaporation in slow cookers — they trap moisture so recipes require less liquid."
- **General conversion ratio.** Multiple sources (Bored of Lunch, GreenTree Co-op, Blue Jean Chef) recommend reducing stovetop liquid by 1/3 to 1/2 when converting to slow cooker. For a brothy soup, the lower end (25–33% reduction) is appropriate because you do want it brothy, just not watery.
- **Pasta absorption is the hidden tightener.** Ditalini added dry in the last 30 minutes will absorb roughly 1.5–2 cups of liquid as it cooks. Multiple user comments across Damn Delicious, Persnickety Plates, and The Country Cook flag this: "the pasta swells after cooking; I had to add extra broth when reheating." The Country Cook's recipe author explicitly says: "Pasta likes to absorb a lot of the liquid so if you like your soup a bit more soupy, be prepared to have an extra can of beef broth handy."
- **Target starting liquid for a ~5-quart fill.** Across surveyed slow-cooker copycats, the median is roughly 4 cups beef broth + 1 28-oz can crushed tomatoes + 1 15-oz can tomato sauce + 2 15-oz cans drained beans. That assumes pasta will be added and absorb. If the user wants a brothier final result, add 1 extra cup of broth at the pasta-addition step rather than the start (avoids over-thinning the long-cook phase).
- **The 33969A's specific fill rule.** Hamilton Beach's manual states explicitly: "Your best results will be achieved using recipes that yield 5 quarts or less" in the 6-qt unit, with a 2-inch headspace for soups so the recipe "can come to a simmer." A 5-quart final target is the ceiling.

### 5. Layering / Dump-and-Go Order — Mostly cargo cult for soup
- **Layering matters for slow cookers in general** — Mashed and The Kitchn document that root vegetables and tough cuts belong at the bottom near the heating element, delicate vegetables and dairy at the top. The Hamilton Beach manual specifically notes: "Place thick onion slices underneath so the meat will not sit on (and cook in) the fat" — for high-fat-content meats.
- **For a fully submerged soup like pasta e fagioli, the convection of the simmering liquid largely equalizes everything.** No recipe in the surveyed set treats layering as critical for this dish. The one defensible practice: put the raw ground beef on the bottom in pinched-off chunks (rather than one solid lump) so heat reaches it first and it has a chance to actually cook through and break apart before the soup phase fully establishes. The Lazy Slow Cooker explicitly recommends placing beef on bottom and adding a small amount of liquid (1/2 cup beef broth) to prevent scorching during the initial warming.
- **Verdict:** Don't make the recipe instructions complicated with layering choreography. "Drop everything in, beef first and broken up, give it a stir" is sufficient.

### 6. Flavor Development Without Browning — Three high-leverage additions
Surveying the copycat field, the additions that show up repeatedly and earn user-comment praise (vs. those that feel like padding):

- **Tomato paste — high leverage, can go in raw.** Multiple slow-cooker tomato-based soup recipes (Real Food Whole Life, The Recipe Rebel, The Chunky Chef, The Magical Slow Cooker) add tomato paste directly to the cold slow cooker without stovetop blooming and report no off-notes. The long cook time and acidity of the tomato-heavy environment cook out raw-paste flavors. Blooming would deepen the flavor by perhaps 10–15% but requires a separate pan — not worth the friction. **Add 2–3 tablespoons of tomato paste straight from the can/tube.**
- **Beef bouillon concentrate (Better Than Bouillon) — replaces or augments beef broth and punches above its weight.** Better Than Bouillon's own published guidance specifically promotes using their bases in slow cookers because they "help build flavors, not dilute them." Replacing 1 cup of beef broth with 1 cup water + 1 tablespoon Better Than Bouillon Roasted Beef Base intensifies the savory backbone meaningfully. Several user commenters across multiple recipes mention using bouillon cubes (Chelsea's Messy Apron explicitly: "crumble the bouillon cube into the soup") as a flavor backstop.
- **Red wine vinegar at the end — non-negotiable for Olive Garden authenticity.** Todd Wilbur's Top Secret Recipes clone, Chelsea's Messy Apron, Salt & Lavender, and Momsdish all use red wine vinegar; it's the signature top-note that distinguishes Olive Garden's version from generic minestrone. Add at the very end (off-heat or on Warm), 1–2 teaspoons per quart of finished soup, stirred in just before serving. Adding it at the start dulls it.
- **Parmesan rind — nice-to-have, not essential.** Multiple surveyed recipes (Damn Delicious, Salt & Lavender, Food52) include a rind during the cook. Taste of Home calls Parmesan rinds "the thrifty ingredient to mind-blowing soup." Real flavor lift is documented, but only if you already have one in the fridge — telling readers to go buy one defeats the dump-and-go ethos.
- **Worcestershire / soy / fish sauce — surveyed but underwhelming for this dish.** A few recipes (Lemons & Zest hamburger soup) use Worcestershire, but it's not characteristic of Olive Garden's profile and risks pushing the broth toward American beef-stew territory. Skip in favor of the bouillon + vinegar combo.
- **Italian seasoning blend over individual herbs.** Five+ surveyed recipes (Cooking Classy explicitly: "If you don't have all the different dried herbs then you can just use an Italian seasoning blend. Use 1 Tbsp.") use either a blend or the four-herb stack (basil, oregano, thyme, marjoram). For dump-and-go, the blend is fine — 1 tablespoon for the recipe is the consensus.
- **A pinch of sugar.** Multiple authors (Cooking Classy, The Recipe Rebel) add a small amount of sugar (1 teaspoon or so) to balance tomato acidity. Olive Garden's version is noticeably sweeter than traditional Italian pasta e fagioli; sugar is one of the ways copycats reach that profile.

### 7. The Olive Garden Copycat Landscape — Convergence and divergence

**Strong convergence (5+ recipes agree):**
- Ground beef, not Italian sausage. Damn Delicious's slow-cooker version uses sausage, which is a deviation; the rest of the slow-cooker copycat field uses ground beef. Olive Garden's actual menu description reads "ground beef." Damn Delicious's choice appears to be a personal preference, not a fidelity move.
- Ditalini pasta specifically. Universal.
- Two-bean blend: cannellini + red kidney.
- Mirepoix base: onion, carrot, celery, garlic.
- Italian seasoning (or basil + oregano + thyme).
- Diced tomatoes + tomato sauce as the tomato base.
- Beef broth as the liquid backbone.
- Brown the meat first (consensus despite the friction cost — see Section 1 for the contrarian case).

**Points of divergence (the recipe needs to pick):**
- **Sausage vs. beef:** Damn Delicious vs. everyone else. Pick beef per the user's stated profile.
- **Liquid quantity:** Ranges from 2 cups (5 Dollar Dinners) to a full 32-oz carton (365 Days of Crockpot) of broth. Wider variance comes from authors' preferred final consistency. Median is ~4 cups for a recipe yielding ~3 quarts pre-pasta.
- **Beans in early vs. with pasta at end:** Split roughly 50/50 (see Section 2).
- **Vinegar type and amount:** Red wine vinegar (Chelsea's Messy Apron, Top Secret Recipes, Salt & Lavender) vs. white vinegar (Wilbur's original clone uses both white and red wine vinegar in some iterations). Red wine vinegar is the more common and more authentic choice.
- **V-8 juice as a Wilbur signature:** Wilbur's original Top Secret Recipes clone specifies V-8 vegetable juice as a hidden ingredient. I Heart Naptime and Food.com's #289500 follow this. Most slow-cooker adaptations drop the V-8 entirely; it's optional but does add an identifiable flavor note if pursuing maximum fidelity.
- **Pasta cooked in the soup vs. separately:** Authors split based on whether they expect leftovers. Roughly half explicitly recommend cooking pasta separately for leftover-keepers.

**Recipes worth examining closely:**
- **Chelsea's Messy Apron (chelseasmessyapron.com/crockpot-pasta-e-fagioli-olive-garden-copycat-recipe-video):** Most rigorous on vinegar timing (split between cooking pasta and finishing soup) and explicitly addresses the leftover-pasta-bloat problem.
- **Top Secret Recipes / Todd Wilbur (topsecretrecipes.com/olive-garden-pasta-e-fagioli-copycat-recipe.html):** Self-described "the only one that will fool in a side-by-side taste test." Heavy user endorsement from former Olive Garden employees in the comments. Stovetop, not slow cooker, but the ingredient ratios are the cleanest reference for what the broth should taste like.
- **Damn Delicious (damndelicious.net/2020/10/27/slow-cooker-olive-garden-pasta-e-fagioli):** Major-traffic slow-cooker version. Useful for comparison but uses sausage instead of beef — adjust for fidelity.
- **The Recipe Critic (therecipecritic.com/slow-cooker-pasta-e-fagioli-soup):** Heavily reviewed slow-cooker copycat; user comments contain useful corrections (most commenters report sautéing the aromatics anyway despite the recipe saying "dump").
- **Cooking Classy (cookingclassy.com/olive-garden-pasta-e-fagioli-soup-copycat-recipe):** Stovetop, but ingredient ratios are heavily user-validated and frequently cited.
- **Persnickety Plates (persnicketyplates.com/slow-cooker-pasta-e-fagioli-olive-garden-copycat):** Clean slow-cooker version, uses 93/7 lean — useful data point on lean-beef performance.
- **365 Days of Crockpot (365daysofcrockpot.com/recipe-for-slow-cooker-copycat-olive):** Long comment history with substantive user feedback specifically about pasta separation and leftover handling.

**User comment patterns to weight heavily:**
- Multiple commenters across multiple recipes note flavors are "muted" in slow-cooker versions and that adding vinegar + a "pinch of salt" at the end fixes it (specifically called out at The Recipe Critic).
- Across recipes that include the standard "brown the beef first" step, a non-trivial number of commenters volunteer that they "just dumped it all in raw and it was fine" — corroborating Section 1.
- The single most common user complaint across all surveyed slow-cooker copycats is "pasta turned to mush in leftovers." Universally addressed by either cooking pasta separately or storing pasta and broth separately.

### 8. Temperature Probe — Irrelevant for this dish, settle and move on
- The 33969A's probe is designed for whole cuts of meat. The official Hamilton Beach manual's Probe Cooking Guide lists target internal temperatures of 145°F to 180°F for beef roast, brisket, turkey breast, whole chicken, ham, pork tenderloin, pork roast, and pork chops — none of which describe soup or ground meat. (A JustAnswer user report for the 33969A specifically notes the user-selectable probe target on this model caps at 180°F.)
- The manual's Probe Mode section instructs inserting the probe "in center of food and not touching bottom of crock." There is no meaningful "center" of a soup — the probe would read broth temperature, which equilibrates to roughly 200°F on HIGH and roughly 195°F on LOW regardless of where the food itself is in its cook. Probe-targeting a soup is meaningless.
- Use Program mode (timer-based) instead. Set the time, let the unit auto-shift to Warm.
- **Final decision: ignore the probe in the recipe entirely. Do not mention it except possibly a single sentence in a FAQ saying "the probe is not used for this recipe."**

### 9. Hold / Serve Behavior
- **Refrigerated shelf life:** 3–5 days is the dominant range across surveyed recipes. The Recipe Critic, Spend With Pennies, and Insanely Good cite 3–4 days; CopyKat Recipes cites 3–5 days. Use 4 days as a safe default.
- **Pasta continues absorbing.** Universally documented. Leftovers thicken meaningfully overnight. Stir in extra broth or water when reheating (Spend With Pennies, Damn Delicious, Persnickety Plates all explicitly call this out).
- **Flavor improves overnight.** Multiple recipe authors and commenters mention day-two soup tastes better — the herbs, vinegar, and beef flavor meld further once cold.
- **For leftover-friendly execution:** Either undercook the pasta by 5 minutes (so it absorbs leftover broth to al dente by day two), or cook pasta separately and combine at the bowl. The latter is the universal recommendation when leftovers are guaranteed, but it violates the one-pot constraint. The recipe should default to in-pot pasta and add a "for guaranteed leftovers, cook the pasta in a separate pot and add to bowls" note.
- **Freezing:** Soup base freezes well up to 3 months. Pasta does not freeze well in the soup — it becomes mushy on thaw. If freezing is anticipated, omit pasta from the freeze portion.

### 10. Common Failure Modes (in approximate order of frequency in user comments)
1. **Pasta mush** — by far the most common. Caused by cooking pasta the full slow-cook duration, by leaving leftovers in the soup, or by adding too much pasta. Fix: pasta in last 30 min on HIGH; remove from heat promptly; for leftovers, separate pasta.
2. **Bland / muted flavor** — second most common. Slow-cooker volatiles don't escape the same way they do in an open pot, but the long cook also dulls the brightness of fresh herbs and acid. Fix: salt at the end (not the start), vinegar at the end, bouillon concentrate for backbone, and consider a small splash of olive oil stirred in just before serving for the "Olive Garden" mouthfeel.
3. **Watery broth** — third most common. Caused by following stovetop liquid quantities verbatim. Fix: reduce starting liquid by 25–33%; tomato paste also helps body.
4. **Grease pooling on top** — fourth most common, almost entirely tied to 80/20 ground beef dumped raw without browning. Fix: use 90/10 or leaner, OR skim with a spoon or paper towel at the end.
5. **Raw-tasting or clumped ground beef** — fifth. Almost always caused by failing to break up the raw beef when loading and not stirring during the cook. Fix: break beef into roughly 1-inch chunks at load and stir once at the halfway point (one justified lid-lift) and again when the pasta goes in.
6. **Beans too soft / dissolved** — minor but real. Caused by adding beans at the start and cooking 8+ hours on LOW. Fix: add beans with the pasta in the final 30 minutes.
7. **Overcooked / dry ground beef** — flagged by ATK. Caused by cooks longer than 8 hours on LOW. Fix: cap at 7–8 hours on LOW; or use 6 hours on LOW + 30 minutes on HIGH for the pasta phase.

## Recommendations: Key Decisions the Synthesis Stage Will Need to Make (trade-offs)

1. **Lean ground beef raw-dump vs. browned-and-drained.** The honest call is raw-dump 90/10 with vigorous load-time break-up and one mid-cook stir. Flavor delta from skillet-browned is small and is fully masked by the tomato-vinegar-herb profile. The friction cost saved is significant (no skillet, no draining, no second dish). **Risk:** Some users will get a slightly grayish, less-caramelized beef appearance. Decision lever: state the leaner-beef requirement clearly and frame it as the price of the no-skillet promise.

2. **Pasta in the pot vs. on the side.** In-pot pasta is one-pot-clean and matches the dump-and-go ethos but guarantees leftover degradation. Side-pasta gives perfect texture forever but requires a second pot. **Decision lever:** Default to in-pot pasta (matches the brief), with an explicit "if you know you'll have leftovers, cook the pasta separately" sidebar. Don't dual-instruct in the main flow.

3. **Starting liquid quantity.** Lean toward the brothier end of the range (4 to 4.5 cups broth + canned tomato liquid) and accept a slightly thicker leftover, OR lean toward the lower end (3 cups) and produce a stew-leaning consistency that's closer to Olive Garden's chunky service style. **Decision lever:** Olive Garden's actual soup is more stew-than-soup. Start lean (3.5–4 cups broth) and have the user add a splash of broth at serving if they want it looser.

4. **Cook time and setting.** Trade-off between 7–8 hours on LOW (cleanest commute-time match for a workday) and 4 hours on HIGH (faster but with somewhat worse vegetable texture and slightly less integrated flavor). Both work. **Decision lever:** Default to 7–8 hours LOW (workday default), provide HIGH timing as a sidebar for evening starts. Pasta phase is always 30 min on HIGH regardless of which path was used for the main cook.

5. **Aromatics — sautéed vs. raw-dumped.** Sautéing onion and garlic before adding is the highest-leverage flavor improvement available short of browning the beef. Many of the user comments on otherwise dump-and-go recipes volunteer that the commenter sautéed the aromatics anyway. But sautéing requires a skillet — the exact friction the brief is trying to avoid. **Decision lever:** Don't sauté in the default recipe. If the resulting flavor is too flat in testing, the smallest acceptable concession is sautéing only the onion and garlic (not the carrot/celery) for 3 minutes — but this should be an "if you have 5 minutes" optional note, not a default step.

## Caveats
- All "consensus" claims in this report are based on roughly 12 to 15 surveyed slow-cooker pasta e fagioli copycat recipes plus ~10 stovetop reference recipes. None of these are independent — many cross-cite each other and may share genetic lineage from Todd Wilbur's 2000s-era Olive Garden clone. The "consensus" is therefore weaker than a 15-recipe count suggests.
- America's Test Kitchen has never published a head-to-head test of raw-dumped vs. browned ground beef in a slow-cooker tomato-broth soup. The strongest argument for raw-dump is therefore inferential (USDA color-doesn't-equal-doneness + multiple food-blog raw-dump recipes with positive user comments + ATK's own evidence that even pre-browned beef degrades in long slow cooks) rather than direct experimental.
- The 33969A's official manual confirms generic operating guidance but contains no model-specific advice on pasta or soup recipes beyond a general "pasta, seafood, milk, cream, or sour cream should be added 2 hours before serving" rule, which is too conservative for ditalini — 30 minutes is sufficient on HIGH and is the cross-recipe consensus.
- Quantitative claims about lid-lift heat loss: the Hamilton Beach manual confirms only that lid removal causes "major heat loss" without quantification. Montana State University Extension's "Cooking With a Slow Cooker" fact sheet quantifies the lower bound: "It can take 20 minutes to regain the heat lost from removing the lid." The widely cited 30-minute upper bound remains a community heuristic.
- This document is structured findings only and not a recipe. Final synthesis should pressure-test the recommended approach (raw 90/10 + dump-and-go + 30-min pasta on HIGH + end-of-cook vinegar) against one round of actual testing before publication.
