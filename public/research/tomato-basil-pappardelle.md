## 1. Pasta: reference doughs

| Source | Formula | Method notes | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Serious Eats** (as reproduced at joeomar.com/recipes/RecipeFreshPasta.html) | 9 oz (~255 g) AP flour, 2 whole eggs (~4 oz), 4 yolks (~2.5 oz), 1 tsp kosher salt | All-purpose flour with extra yolks | The page's author computed this as about 1.41 flour-to-egg weight. Per 100 g flour that is ~70 g egg, a rich, tender dough. |
| **America's Test Kitchen "Fresh Pasta"** (americastestkitchen.com/recipes/12328) | 283 g (2 cups) AP flour, 2 eggs + 6 yolks | Food processor, ~45 sec, until "soft and barely tacky." If it sticks, add flour up to ¼ cup, 1 Tbsp at a time. | Built to be tender and workable. |
| **ATK "Fresh Pasta Without a Machine"** (…/6948) | Same flour and eggs, plus 2 Tbsp olive oil and 1 Tbsp salt (the salt is mostly for the cooking water) | Hand-rolled. Roll with the pin from the center out, in one stroke. Roll to ~20 × 6 in, thin enough that your fingers' outline shows through. Minimal dusting flour, because too much causes snapback. | This is the rolling-pin fallback. |
| **Missy Robbins** (*Pasta*, 2021; ctpublic.org copy) | 500 g 00 flour + 454 g yolks (24–26) | Flour "fortress" with a well no wider than 5–6 in. Beat the yolks only lightly. At Lilia she mixes in a stand mixer and adds a few drops of water if dry. Knead by hand, rest ≥30 min. Roll through each setting twice, until nearly transparent. | All-yolk dough: ~91 g yolk per 100 g flour. It is very silky, bright yellow and delicate. |
| **Giada (Food Network)** | 1½ cups 00 + ¼ cup semolina, 1 whole egg + 8 yolks, ¼ tsp salt | A yolk-heavy dough made explicitly for pappardelle | Semolina adds bite. |
| **"1 egg per 100 g flour"** (Hazan-style, widely quoted) | 100 g flour : 1 large egg | The lean, chewier Bolognese ratio | The standard against which the rich doughs are rich. |

Other formulas from the same roundup page include 3:1 00 to semolina, and 1 egg + 1 yolk per 150 g flour. A semolina blend is also common at Pasta Social Club: 350 g 00 + 5 Tbsp semola rimacinata + 4 eggs + 1–2 yolks.

**Consensus**
- Weigh the flour.
- Use extra yolks for ribbon pastas such as pappardelle. The fat from yolks limits gluten development, which gives a silky, tender texture and a more delicate dough (mosthungry.com).
- Rest at least 30 minutes.
- Dust with semolina or rice flour, not plain flour, to prevent sticking.

**Disagreements**
- **Flour:** 00 gives softer, more tender pasta. Adding semolina gives more bite, and Burnt Butter Table says 00 alone is "a lot softer." AP flour works. ATK and Serious Eats both use AP.
- **Egg ratio:** the range runs from ~50 g egg per 100 g flour (Hazan-style) to ~91 g yolk per 100 g flour (Robbins).
- **Oil and salt in the dough:** ATK adds oil. Most Italian sources don't.
- **Mixing method:** food processor (ATK), mixer (Robbins at Lilia), or hand-mixed well (Robbins' book).

**Rolling and cutting.** The pasta-machine setting is machine-dependent. Burnt Butter Table rolls pappardelle to setting 7 on a KitchenAid roller, the second-thinnest. I would aim for about 1 mm, or a hand visible through the sheet. For cutting, from background knowledge: fold the floured sheet loosely, cut ~2–3 cm ribbons, shake them out and nest them on semolina.

**My recommended dough for 4 servings (adapted by me, not a quoted recipe):**
- 300 g 00 flour, plus 50 g semolina rimacinata.
- 2 whole eggs plus 5 yolks, about 190–200 g total. Weigh the eggs and adjust.
- A pinch of salt (¼ tsp).
- Rest 30–60 minutes wrapped, at room temperature or in the fridge.
- That makes ~550 g of dough, about 125–135 g per person, which is generous.
- For a lighter portion, scale to 250 g flour.

## 2. Sauce: reference approaches

**Marcella Hazan: tomatoes, butter, onion** (gustiamo.com, thekitchn.com, southernfoodandfun.com)
- 1 can (28 oz / 800 g) whole peeled San Marzano tomatoes.
- 5 Tbsp (70 g) unsalted butter.
- 1 small onion, halved and peeled.
- Salt.
- Simmer for about 45 minutes, mashing the tomatoes with a spoon. Discard the onion.
- Kitchn's rationale: the butter rounds out acidity without the "sawing off" that sugar does. This is the best-documented, most reproducible baseline.
- The source for the sauce's origin is Victor Hazan, who quotes Marcella's mother sautéing onion in butter until deep gold before adding tomatoes.

**Pomodoro-style, olive oil base**
- Billy Parisi: 3 × 28 oz cans (or 5 lb fresh tomatoes) pureed, 2 Tbsp oil, ½ onion, 4 garlic cloves, 12–15 basil leaves. Stew 30 minutes, then add the basil at the end.
- Foodiecrush: tear the basil with your fingers so it doesn't oxidize the way knife-cut basil does. Add it when cooking is nearly complete. (This is a recipe-site assertion, not a tested claim.)
- Marcellina in Cucina: puree plus oil and garlic, simmered for 60–70 minutes, with basil in the final 10 minutes. The pasta goes in with a splash of pasta water for 1 minute.
- Ambitious Kitchen: it blends half a cup of pasta water into the sauce with an immersion blender, then folds in basil.

**Tomato choice**
- ATK's whole-canned-tomato test winner: Cento San Marzano Certified (DOP). Tasters called it "bright, fresh, and sweet" with a soft, velvety texture.
- A HuffPost tasting rated Cento only a B, citing excess sweetness and a mineral aftertaste. It preferred Simpson Brands, a domestic "San Marzano-style" can.
- So DOP is not a guarantee.
- Serious Eats' Gritzer, quoted in a Yahoo roundup, argues that whole peeled tomatoes are the most versatile can, because any other can can be made from them.

**Consensus**
- Use whole peeled canned tomatoes.
- Add basil late.
- Tear basil.
- Use fat (butter or oil) for roundness.
- Season lightly.

**Disagreements**
- Butter and onion (Hazan) versus oil and garlic (most pomodoro recipes).
- Pureeing (Parisi, Ambitious Kitchen) versus hand-crushing (Hazan).
- Sauce simmer time: 30 to 70 minutes.

**Not retrieved, from background knowledge, verify:**
- Basil's aroma compounds are volatile and fade with heat. Add most of the basil at the end, off heat.
- Blanching and shocking basil gives a bright, stable green oil.
- Blending aerates the sauce and turns it orange.
- Baking soda and sugar are common fixes for acidity, but butter and longer cooking are gentler.

## 3. Finishing and mantecatura

- ATK (americastestkitchen.com/articles/7773) describes the technique. Cook the pasta *al chiodo* ("to the nail", about halfway). Then combine it in a pan with the sauce, fat and plenty of starchy water. Simmer until the pasta is al dente and the liquid reduces to a creamy sauce.
- ATK says the pasta absorbs water while releasing more starch, which helps emulsify the water and fat. They also scaled the cooking water down to 2 quarts per pound, for the same starch in less water.
- Tasting Table's cue: the sauce is ready when a spoon dragged through it leaves a track that doesn't refill quickly.
- It says to add pasta water in stages, with a drizzle of olive oil or grated Parmigiano to help the emulsion.
- Serious Eats' test, via the aldente.substack.com summary: the starchiest water gave the most stable emulsion, even in plain olive oil. I did not retrieve the original article.

**Fresh pasta note.** Fresh pappardelle cooks in about 1–2 minutes (from background knowledge). Pull it a few seconds early and finish it in the sauce.

## 4. Recommended approach for 4 servings

*This is my synthesis and not a single source's recipe. Quantities are starting points.*

**Sauce (can be made a day ahead)**
- Simmer 800 g (one 28 oz can) San Marzano tomatoes with 70 g butter, 1 halved onion and a pinch of salt for 45 minutes (Hazan).
- Optionally, add 1 Tbsp tomato paste, fried in 2 Tbsp olive oil with a crushed garlic clove, for depth.
- Optionally, blend lightly.
- Add about 15 g of basil stems for infusion and remove them at the end.

**Basil**
- Keep the basil in two layers: stems in the sauce, and about 30 g of torn leaves stirred in off heat.
- Optional basil oil: blanch the leaves for 10–15 seconds, shock them, blend with ~80 ml olive oil, strain, and drizzle on at the end. This is a visual and aromatic upgrade. It is not essential.

**Cooking**
1. Boil 4–5 L of water with ~10 g salt per liter.
2. Warm the sauce in a wide stainless or cast-iron skillet.
3. Cook the pasta 60–90 seconds, then reserve ~250 ml of water.
4. Transfer the pasta to the sauce with about 60–100 ml of water and a knob of butter or olive oil.
5. Toss over medium-high heat for 30–60 seconds until glossy and clinging.
6. Add the basil and ~40 g Parmigiano off the heat.

**Worth it**
- Weighing the ingredients.
- Resting the dough.
- A high-yolk dough.
- The finish in the pan with starchy water.
- Basil added late.

**Skip, or treat as optional**
- Laminated basil leaves in the dough. I found no tested source for the flavor payoff, and the basil mostly adds looks.
- Grilled or roasted tomatoes, unless fresh tomatoes are at peak season.

## Common Mistakes, Pro Tips & Regional Authenticity

## 1. Mistakes and tips

### Sourced
- Pappardelle are long, broad, flat ribbons of traditionally egg pasta, and they originate in Tuscany.
- Their wide shape and rough, porous surface suit robust, meaty sauces and ragùs.
- The same source says pappardelle al ragù di cinghiale (wild boar) is the traditional Tuscan dish associated with this pasta.
- This matters for your dish. Tomato-basil is a lighter dressing than the shape traditionally gets. It works only if the sauce is rich and clingy enough to coat a wide ribbon, so build body with butter or oil and starchy water.

### Unsourced

**Dough**
- **Ratio:** Use about 100 g 00 flour per whole egg (Hazan's ratio). For richer dough, replace some whole eggs with yolks. I've seen roughly 400 g flour, 2 whole eggs and 4 yolks used for a yolk-rich dough, but I haven't verified that.
- **Weigh the eggs.** Egg size varies, so weigh them. Hydration of about 50–55% of flour weight is a reasonable target.
- **Dough too dry:** A shaggy dough is normal at first. Wrap it and rest it 30–60 minutes before judging, because the flour keeps hydrating. Don't add water early.
- **Dough too wet:** It sticks and tears in the machine. Fix this with a light flour dusting, not a lot of extra flour.
- **Under-resting:** The dough springs back and tears when you roll it. Rest it at least 30 minutes, covered, at room temperature.
- **Rolling thickness:** Roll thin enough to see your hand's shadow through the sheet. On a machine this is usually about the second-to-last setting. Too thick gives a dense, doughy pasta.
- **Cutting:** Flour the sheet, fold it loosely (don't press it flat), and cut ribbons about 2–3 cm wide. Toss them with semolina or flour and nest them. Cover the nests so they don't dry out and crack.

**Cooking**
- **Water and timing:** Use plenty of well-salted water, about 10 g salt per liter. Fresh pappardelle takes roughly 1–2 minutes, so pull it while still slightly firm.
- **Overcooking:** This is the most common error. The pasta finishes cooking in the sauce.
- **Pasta water:** Always reserve a mug of pasta water before draining.

**Sauce**
- **Burnt garlic:** It turns bitter. Add it to oil that isn't hot, or add it later.
- **Tinny or acidic flavor:** Cook the sauce longer. Butter softens acidity. A small pinch of sugar can help, but use it sparingly.
- **Watery sauce:** Reduce until the oil starts to separate, or until it holds a line when you drag a spoon through it.
- **Basil:** Heat dulls and blackens it. Add most of it at the end, off the heat. Tear it rather than cutting it, which helps limit bruising and browning. A blanched basil oil keeps the color bright.

**Finishing (the main difference between mediocre and excellent)**
- Finish the drained pasta in the sauce over medium-high heat for 30–60 seconds. Add pasta water a splash at a time (about 60–120 ml for 4 servings) and toss or stir vigorously.
- Add a knob of butter or a drizzle of olive oil so the sauce emulsifies and turns glossy.
- Add the cheese off the heat, so it doesn't clump.
- Aim for lightly coated ribbons, not a pool of sauce.

## 2. Authenticity and variations

**Sourced**
- Pappardelle is Tuscan in origin and associated with game ragùs, as above.

**Unsourced, from memory**
- **Emilia-Romagna:** The region is known for tagliatelle and for a more rigorous egg-rich *sfoglia*. Hand-rolled with a *mattarello* is the traditional method.
- **Tuscany:** Pappardelle are served with boar, hare or duck ragù. Tomato-basil in Tuscany is more usually *pappa al pomodoro* or a simple *sugo* than a pasta dressing.
- **Southern Italy:** Tomato-basil pasta is a southern tradition, with Naples famous for it. It usually uses dried semolina pasta (spaghetti or similar), not egg ribbons. Sicily has *pesto alla trapanese* (tomato, almond, basil).
- **Hazan's sauce:** Her classic sauce is tomatoes, butter and a halved onion, simmered about 45 minutes, with the onion discarded. I recall roughly 2 cups of canned tomatoes to 5 tablespoons of butter and half an onion, but please verify this.
- **Italian-American versions:** These tend to be heavier on garlic, sugar and dried herbs, and often sauce more generously.
- **What purists insist on:** Good tomatoes (San Marzano or similar, or peak-season fresh), restrained seasoning, and basil as an aromatic. They also want the pasta finished in the pan, and they avoid cream or too much garlic.

**A note on the pairing:** A tomato-basil sauce on egg pappardelle is a modern, non-traditional pairing. It is delicious and defensible, but it isn't a "regional classic." Present it that way in the recipe.

**Recommended direction:** For the "Hard" version, I'd suggest:
- Hand-cut egg pappardelle.
- A butter-and-tomato sauce (Hazan-style, possibly with some roasted tomato).
- Basil oil plus torn basil at the end.
- A proper *mantecatura* finish.

Skip basil-laminated pasta unless you want the look. It adds little flavor.
